\begin{abstract}
Cosmo-Local Credit is a product family built around the \textbf{Commitment Pooling Protocol (CPP)}. CPP describes how independently governed Commitment Pools can curate redeemable commitments, publish exchange-rate methods and limits, hold inventory, and enable accountable exchange.

A \textbf{Commitment Pool} is the governed arrangement. A current Protocol v1.1.0 \textbf{\texttt{SwapPool}} is one contract component: a token vault and direct-swap engine. A deployment can connect that contract to registries, quoters, Pool token-balance caps, fee policies, and other services.

Cosmo-local means sharing open standards, software, and knowledge globally while keeping issuance, fulfillment, governance, and real-world accountability local. An issuer remains responsible for its voucher. A Pool Steward remains responsible for Pool rules and any guarantee it expressly assumes. Neither the CLC App nor GEF automatically guarantees a voucher, Pool, value, route, liquidity position, or outcome.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Design model}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Design model}

CPP uses four broad functions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Curation:} decide which vouchers or other assets are admitted.
\item \textbf{Valuation:} publish the method used to produce Pool exchange rates or quotes.
\item \textbf{Limitation:} apply current Pool token-balance caps or other separately implemented risk controls.
\item \textbf{Exchange:} hold inventory and execute Pool swaps under disclosed fees and bounds.
\end{itemize}
A deployment may add routing, monitoring, liquidity support, guarantees, reserves, insurance, or governance services only under separately published policies. Listing, curation, a limit, a reserve, or a blockchain record does not by itself establish issuer capacity, fulfillment, endorsement, a guarantee, or insurance.

\section*{Values and design principles}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Values and design principles}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Care for people:} support individual and collective well-being.
\item \textbf{Care for the environment:} reduce ecological harm and support regenerative practice.
\item \textbf{Fairness:} promote secure and equitable access to resources, knowledge, and care.
\item \textbf{Reciprocity:} share risk, cost, responsibility, and surplus under disclosed rules.
\item \textbf{Non-dominance:} resist concentrated control over people, data, finance, knowledge, and material resources.
\item \textbf{Resilience:} help communities prepare for and adapt to economic, political, and climate disruptions.
\item \textbf{Local accountability:} keep real-world fulfillment and legal responsibility with identified parties.
\item \textbf{Forkability:} allow compatible communities and deployments to leave shared registries or services without erasing valid balances or obligations.
\end{itemize}
The digital layer is shared memory and coordination infrastructure. It is not a substitute for relationships, issuer performance, local governance, or legal accountability.

\subsection*{Current direct Pool flow}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Current direct Pool flow}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A holder reviews a voucher, its issuer, its terms, and associated Offerings.
\item A Pool admits supported assets and publishes its configuration and responsible authorities.
\item The holder obtains a quote and authorizes a direct Pool swap.
\item \texttt{SwapPool} performs on-chain swap settlement and accounts for Pool and protocol fees.
\item If the holder later chooses \textbf{``Redeem''} in the App, the App prepares a transfer to the token owner as redemption presentment.
\item The issuer separately performs the promised fulfillment and records discharge so fulfilled units cannot be reused.
\end{enumerate}
Taking inventory from a Pool is a swap. It is redemption only if that Pool has separately assumed the issuer role and fulfills the applicable voucher terms.

\section*{1. Commitment Pooling Protocol (CPP): the core primitive}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{1. Commitment Pooling Protocol (CPP): the core primitive}

\textbf{Mental model:} A Commitment Pool is a governed arrangement for admitting vouchers or other assets, publishing exchange rules, holding inventory, and enabling swaps. Holders later present vouchers to their issuers for real-world fulfillment. Pool exchange and issuer fulfillment are separate lifecycles.

CPP coordinates value through clearly described commitments. The model is discussed in \href{https://willruddick.substack.com/p/grassroots-economics-the-book-is}{Grassroots Economics: Reflection and Practice}.

\subsection*{1.1 What is a commitment?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.1 What is a commitment?}

A commitment is an identified party's promise of future delivery---for example food, transport, labor, storage, or another lawful good, service, benefit, or performance. A \textbf{voucher} is a token or record represented as that commitment under published terms.

The token contract records digital mechanics. Voucher terms identify the issuer, Offering, capacity, place, timing, restrictions, presentment, fulfillment, complaints, and discharge process.

\subsection*{1.2 What is a Commitment Pool?}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.2 What is a Commitment Pool?}

A Commitment Pool is the governed arrangement. It can be stewarded by an individual, cooperative, community group, public agency, federation, multisig, service operator, or another accountable structure.

Relevant roles and authorities include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Pool Steward:} publishes and administers Pool rules and any expressly assumed guarantee;
\item \textbf{Pool owner:} holds current \texttt{SwapPool} owner powers;
\item \textbf{proxy administrator:} can upgrade a proxied implementation;
\item \textbf{dependency controllers:} govern configured registries, quoters, limiters, or fee components;
\item \textbf{route finder or operator:} may discover quotes or, in a future implementation, execute a separately authorized route; and
\item \textbf{guarantor:} assumes a defined obligation only through published, funded terms.
\end{itemize}
CPP groups Pool functions into four concepts:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Curation:} admit supported tokens or vouchers.
\item \textbf{Valuation:} publish the method used for an exchange rate or quote.
\item \textbf{Limitation:} apply current Pool token-balance caps or other separately implemented controls.
\item \textbf{Exchange:} hold inventory, execute swaps, account for fees, and emit transaction records.
\end{itemize}
Protocol v1.1.0 implements these functions through \texttt{SwapPool} and optional dependencies. Its current \texttt{Limiter} caps a token balance at a Pool; it does not provide rolling, per-account, or network-wide swap limits. Its current \texttt{SwapRouter} calculates multi-Pool quotes; it does not execute swaps.

The wider proposed CPP design may add rolling limits, account controls, execution routers, HTLC or escrow paths, and batch netting. Those are proposed components, not descriptions of the current limiter or router.

\subsection*{1.3 Current direct-swap logic}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.3 Current direct-swap logic}

A current direct Pool swap:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item checks the optional registry for the input and output tokens;
\item measures the input received;
\item obtains a quote from the configured quoter or applies raw-unit parity;
\item checks the resulting Pool token balance against the optional limiter;
\item calculates the Pool fee and any additional protocol fee;
\item checks available output inventory;
\item transfers the protocol fee and output and accounts for the Pool fee; and
\item emits swap events.
\end{enumerate}
The quote is a transaction parameter, not proof of issuer capacity, redemption value, fair value, cash convertibility, or a guarantee.

\subsection*{1.4 Wider use}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{1.4 Wider use}

Commitment Pools can support community exchange, production, mutual aid, public programs, and other accountable structures. A separately documented credit product could use a voucher as collateral or a repayment instrument, but it would require supplemental and transaction-specific terms. An ordinary send, Pool deposit, Pool swap, redemption presentment, fulfillment, or discharge is not automatically a loan or repayment.

CPP is intended for accountable exchange and auditable transaction records, not speculative churn. On-chain records still do not prove real-world fulfillment or social impact.


\section*{2. The accounting shift: from assets to trust}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{2. The accounting shift: from assets to trust}

Traditional finance begins with \textbf{Assets - Liabilities = Equity}. CPP reframes this for a commitment economy:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Acceptance capacity:} how much of an issuer's commitments a Pool or network is still willing to accept.
\item \textbf{Outstanding commitments:} issued promises that remain unfulfilled and are held by others.
\item \textbf{Fulfillment capacity:} the issuer's evidenced ability to perform those promises.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{Illustrative commitment-capacity relationship:}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Illustrative commitment-capacity relationship:}

Acceptance capacity - outstanding commitments = remaining acceptance capacity

This conceptual identity can inform Pool risk limits: unlimited issuance does not imply unlimited acceptance. It is not a balance-sheet identity or a statement that every Voucher is legally a debt or loan.

\textbf{Example:} A transporter issues ``100 rides'' in Vouchers. A Pool accepts at most 40 rides worth at a time. If 15 accepted rides remain outstanding, the Pool has capacity to accept up to 25 additional rides under that policy. The calculation does not itself create a loan or guarantee fulfillment.



\section*{3. Fulfillment, discharge, and exchange activity}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{3. Fulfillment, discharge, and exchange activity}

A Pool swap changes which addresses hold assets. Voucher fulfillment occurs when the issuer performs the published commitment. Discharge records the fulfilled units so they cannot be presented again. These events must not be collapsed into one use of ``settlement.''

The proposed measurement framework uses separate records for:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item completed Pool swap volume;
\item valid redemption presentments;
\item confirmed issuer fulfillment;
\item discharge;
\item outstanding eligible commitments; and
\item measured Pool inventory.
\end{itemize}
A proposed commitment-discharge velocity can compare fulfilled value during a period with average outstanding eligible commitment value, but only where both use the same disclosed valuation method. Token supply is not a sufficient denominator: it can include issuer inventory, expired or inaccessible units, tests, and balances that do not represent an outstanding third-party commitment.

A separate Pool swap-activity ratio can compare completed Pool swap value with measured Pool inventory. It describes exchange activity, not issuer fulfillment.

Liquidity may improve inventory availability and make exchanges easier. It does not guarantee that issuers will perform, that contributors will recover assets, or that a Pool quote represents redemption or cash value. Any liquidity-provider rights require separate published terms.

See \href{https://docs.cosmolocal.credit/white-paper/appendix-a-math-box}{Appendix A} for definitions and \href{https://docs.cosmolocal.credit/white-paper/appendix-c-kpi-definitions}{Appendix C} for the proposed measurement specification.


\section*{4. Reusable forward-style collateral}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{4. Reusable forward-style collateral}

A separately documented credit facility might accept fungible, transferable vouchers as collateral or as a repayment instrument. If it did:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Pool admission could make the collateral more discoverable;
\item additional lawful presentment and fulfillment options could support repayment;
\item limits, inventory, valuation, liquidity, issuer-performance, and default risks would remain; and
\item no route, fulfillment, repayment, value, or recovery would be guaranteed.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{4.1 Producer credit loop}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{4.1 Producer credit loop}

A future CLC-compatible service could support producer credit only when the parties establish a distinct facility and expressly present the transaction as a repayable advance. Its transaction terms would identify the creditor and debtor and state:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the advance and repayment amounts;
\item due dates, interest, fees, and permitted collateral use;
\item assignment and any change in the creditor or claim holder;
\item how voucher fulfillment is valued and evidenced for repayment accounting;
\item partial repayment, remaining amounts, default, and discharge; and
\item available remedies under applicable law.
\end{itemize}
An illustrative flow is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item A producer or service provider issues a voucher representing a commitment to future output, such as ten taxi rides, 50 kilograms of maize, or ten labor-hours.
\item A Pool Steward admits that voucher and publishes the Pool's exchange-rate method, limits, fees, inventory rules, and any expressly assumed protection.
\item A lender or liquidity program separately provides an advance under written transaction terms and accepts the producer's vouchers as collateral or an agreed repayment instrument.
\item Holders may transfer the vouchers, swap them through compatible Pools, or present them to the issuer.
\item The issuer's confirmed fulfillment satisfies the voucher commitment. It reduces the separate credit balance only to the extent, at the value, and upon the evidence specified in the credit terms.
\item Records distinguish voucher fulfillment from credit accounting and identify any partial repayment, remaining amount, assignment, default, or discharge.
\end{enumerate}
This structure could permit agreed in-kind repayment while preserving separate records for the voucher and credit obligations. It does not arise from an ordinary transfer, deposit, Pool swap, redemption presentment, fulfillment, or discharge.

\textbf{Illustrative example:} A producer receives a \$1,000 advance under terms stating that confirmed fulfillment of specified maize vouchers is credited against the repayment amount using a stated valuation method. The lender applies a credit only after receiving the evidence required by those terms. If the credit terms do not make that connection, acquiring, transferring, swapping, presenting, or fulfilling the voucher does not affect the advance.


\section*{5. From isolated Pools to a federated network}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{5. From isolated Pools to a federated network}

Current Protocol v1.1.0 supports direct execution through one \texttt{SwapPool} and provides a quote-only \texttt{SwapRouter}. It does not execute multi-hop routes, HTLCs, escrow routes, batch netting, or cross-network clearing.

This chapter proposes how independently governed Pools could coordinate without giving up their own admission, valuation, limit, fee, inventory, authorization, and governance rules.

\subsection*{5.1 Separate exchange and fulfillment measures}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.1 Separate exchange and fulfillment measures}

Federation might improve access to inventory, but it would not merge the voucher and exchange lifecycles. Any implementation would measure these events separately:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item a route is quoted;
\item one or more Pool swaps execute and settle on-chain;
\item a holder presents voucher units to the issuer;
\item the issuer fulfills the commitment; and
\item fulfilled units are discharged.
\end{enumerate}
More quoted or executed routes do not prove more fulfillment. Reports would state the cohort, period, assets, valuation method and timestamp, exclusions, corrections, and off-chain evidence required by Appendix C.

\textbf{Illustrative route:} A school holds maize vouchers but needs transport vouchers. A route service identifies compatible Pool inventories. Execution would succeed only if every separately authorized hop remained within its quote bounds, limits, fees, inventory, and policy. The resulting swaps would not prove that either issuer later fulfilled its voucher commitments.

\subsection*{5.2 Proposed routing and rebalancing services}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.2 Proposed routing and rebalancing services}

A future route service could support two distinct activities.

\textbf{Participant-initiated execution.} Given input and output assets, an amount, and user constraints, the service could identify a path and prepare execution. Each hop would have its own responsible Pool, quote, authorization, fees, limits, inventory, and receipt. Atomic batches, HTLCs, and escrow are possible future execution choices, not current Protocol behavior.

\textbf{Opt-in Pool rebalancing.} Pool Stewards could publish inventory targets, allowed counterparties, asset classes, quote-deviation bounds, and per-period limits. An accountable service could search for compatible cycles or chains and execute only the authorized intents.

Rebalancing would be opt-in. A Pool could allow participant routes while refusing outbound rebalancing, or it could enable only selected assets, counterparties, and amounts. Every executed hop would produce a receipt, and any service fee would be disclosed separately from Pool and Protocol fees.

\subsubsection*{5.2.1 Confederation and interoperability}

Independent deployments could operate their own registries, interfaces, route services, and policy profiles while choosing compatible data and receipt standards. Cross-profile execution would remain deployment-dependent.

A compatible profile would:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item identify its registry roots, service operators, controllers, and applicable terms;
\item disclose allowed and denied counterparties, assets, adapters, and routes;
\item apply every participating Pool's authorization, limits, fees, and inventory constraints;
\item preserve per-hop quote-to-receipt evidence; and
\item allow otherwise functional Pools to leave or select another registry without erasing balances or issuer obligations.
\end{itemize}
Compatibility can increase available exchange paths and reduce dependency on one registry or operator. It does not make the network, CLC App, GEF, or another Pool responsible for an issuer's fulfillment.

\subsection*{5.3 Proposed network-rake and service-fee model}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.3 Proposed network-rake and service-fee model}

Current Protocol v1.1.0 charges a Pool fee and, when configured, an additional Protocol fee on a direct Pool swap. Those current fees remain distinct.

A future program could separately receive:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item a \textbf{network rake}, defined as a stated share of participating Pools' collected Pool fees; and
\item a \textbf{routing or service fee}, charged for an identified future service.
\end{enumerate}
The proposed network rake is not an additional percentage applied to the full swap amount after already counting the Pool fee. For Pool \texttt{p}:

\[
\tau_p = f_p \cdot r_p
\]

where \texttt{f\_p} is the Pool-fee rate and \texttt{r\_p} is the proposed share of that Pool fee allocated to the network program.

For a measured period:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{gross Pool fees} are the sum of each Pool's actual collected Pool fees;
\item \textbf{network-rake receipts} are the stated shares of those collected fees;
\item \textbf{service-fee receipts} are separately collected routing or service fees; and
\item \textbf{program fee receipts} equal network-rake receipts plus service-fee receipts.
\end{itemize}
No category is counted twice. Current Protocol fees are not included unless a separately adopted policy lawfully redirects actual Protocol-fee receipts into the future program.

For an aggregate approximation, let:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{Q\_swap} be the value of executed Pool swaps for the defined cohort and period; and
\item \texttt{$\tau$} be the effective rate of the proposed network rake and separately identified service fees over that executed swap value.
\end{itemize}
Then:

\[
F \approx \tau \cdot Q_{\mathrm{swap}}
\]

This is an analytical approximation, not a promise of revenue. Every input requires a stated cohort, period, unit, valuation timestamp, exclusions, and correction policy.

\subsubsection*{5.3.1 Cash-eligible and in-kind receipts}

Fees may arrive in cash-eligible fungible assets or in vouchers and other in-kind assets. In-kind receipts cannot automatically pay cash expenses or coverage claims. Any exchange or conversion would require authority, available inventory, disclosed venues, bounds, and actual execution.

Let \texttt{$\chi$} be the realized share of fee receipts that is cash-eligible after policy restrictions, failed conversions, and slippage. Cash-usable receipts are:

\[
F_{\mathrm{cash}} \approx \chi \cdot F
\]

Budget and break-even analysis would use realized \texttt{F\_cash}, not gross quoted fees or the face value of in-kind inventory. A future program would report gross Pool fees, network-rake receipts, service fees, asset composition, conversion results, and cash-usable receipts separately.

\subsection*{5.4 Proposed liquidity programs}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{5.4 Proposed liquidity programs}

A future, separately documented liquidity program could allocate assets to designated Pools or routing services. Current \texttt{SwapPool} contracts do not mint Pool shares or automatically create repayment, withdrawal, reward, governance, or profit rights.

Any program would publish:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the responsible entity and participating Pool Stewards;
\item contributed assets and whether the transfer is repayable, withdrawable, donated, or endowed;
\item custody and technical-control arrangements;
\item permitted uses, limits, lockups, withdrawal gates, and loss allocation;
\item fee or incentive eligibility and whether the amount may be zero;
\item reporting, conflicts, complaints, and remedies; and
\item migration, termination, and treatment of remaining assets and obligations.
\end{itemize}
Material risks include inventory that is difficult to exchange or fulfill, low cash eligibility, issuer non-performance, contract or provider failures, governance changes, and restrictions on exit. Limits, reserves, receipts, and dashboards may reduce or reveal some risks; they do not eliminate loss.

For an ex-post analytical metric, let:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{$\phi$} be the realized fraction of program fee receipts allocated under the program's adopted terms; and
\item \texttt{K} be the measured value of assets covered by the program under one stated method.
\end{itemize}
Then:

\[
\mathrm{FeeFlow}_{LP} \approx \frac{\phi \cdot F}{K} = \frac{\phi \cdot \tau \cdot Q_{\mathrm{swap}}}{K}
\]

This metric describes realized fee flow per measured program asset. It is not APY, a forecast, a dividend, or a guaranteed return. Reports would keep swap volume, redemption presentment, issuer fulfillment, discharge, holding duration, losses, withdrawals, and fee receipts separate.


\section*{6. Proposed network-level liquidity and governance}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6. Proposed network-level liquidity and governance}

Experience from the historic Sarafu Network motivated research into two wider design questions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item how independently governed Pools could coordinate liquidity and routing services; and
\item how shared services could remain accountable as participation grows.
\end{enumerate}
One proposed CLC design introduces a \textbf{proposed CLC Network Pool} as a network-level clearing and budget-coordination arrangement and a \textbf{proposed CLC governance token}. Neither exists in the current App or Protocol v1.1.0. Other deployments could use cooperatives, public agencies, federations, multisigs, service operators, or other accountable structures without adopting either proposal.

\subsection*{6.1 Downside controls and optionality}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{6.1 Downside controls and optionality}

Current Protocol v1.1.0 can apply Pool token-balance caps and inventory checks. These controls constrain selected contract actions; they do not prevent issuer non-performance, loss of value, key loss, contract failure, or every other loss.

A future deployment could add circuit breakers, timelocks, reserves, guarantees, or insurance. Any protection would need an identified responsible party, covered event, funding, cap, exclusions, duration, evidence, claims process, and applicable terms.

Proposed governance should keep registry and service powers narrow and reviewable. Ordinary actions can use notice, delay, reason publication, appeal, and correction processes. Emergency actions should produce incident records and expire or receive review under an adopted policy.

Compatible route discovery could make more exchanges possible across Pools. Multi-hop execution and netting would require additional software, authorization, bounds, failure handling, and governance. More quoted or executed swaps would not by itself prove voucher fulfillment or social impact.


\section*{7. Proposed CLC stewardship and governance assets}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{7. Proposed CLC stewardship and governance assets}

This chapter presents one optional future governance and liquidity-coordination design. The proposed CLC governance token, stCLC, sCLC, governance vault, insurance fund, Waterfall, gauges, mandates, fee-credit windows, and external-market programs are not part of the current CLC App or Protocol v1.1.0. Each would require an implementation, accountable operator, published policy and terms, security review, and applicable legal approval.

CPP does not require this token model. Compatible deployments could instead use cooperatives, public agencies, federations, multisigs, services, or other accountable structures.

\subsection*{7.1 Purpose}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{7.1 Purpose}

If adopted, the proposed governance layer could:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item coordinate shared registries and route services;
\item allocate approved liquidity mandates across independently governed Pools;
\item administer an optional, expressly scoped, funded coverage program;
\item maintain common infrastructure and observability;
\item recognize contributors under published eligibility and anti-gaming rules; and
\item preserve review, appeal, transparency, and credible exit as participation grows.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{7.2 Proposed governance-asset model}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{7.2 Proposed governance-asset model}

The design contains three distinct proposed assets:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Proposed CLC governance token:} a transferable base governance asset under adopted issuance, custody, voting, and compliance rules.
\item \textbf{stCLC:} a non-transferable vote-escrow receipt minted when the proposed CLC governance token is locked. It would represent time-bounded governance power and could permit disclosed delegation.
\item \textbf{sCLC:} an epoch-scoped authorization or incentive asset issued under policy. It could support capped fee-credit access or approved liquidity and routing incentives and could expire or be burned at epoch end.
\end{enumerate}
Neither stCLC nor sCLC would be equity, a dividend, a residual claim, a promise of return, or a community voucher. An adopted policy could set sCLC issuance and fee-credit access to zero in any epoch.

Governance lockups, minimum durations, cooldowns, delegation, concentration caps, and critical-action thresholds would be published before use. Unlocked proposed CLC governance tokens would not vote under this design.

\subsubsection*{7.2.1 Illustrative supply and allocation}

The illustrative total proposed CLC governance-token supply is \textbf{500,000,000}. This is a design parameter, not a current issuance, offer, valuation, or promise of liquidity.

An illustrative allocation is:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{15\% --- Grassroots Economics Foundation:} permanently staked, non-transferable, and bound to an identified GEF multisig under published signer and rotation rules.
\item \textbf{15\% --- core team and early partners:} staked during a policy-set cliff and linear vesting period of 24 months, with voting and transfer rules disclosed in advance.
\item \textbf{30\% --- private endowments:} recognition for early contributors providing approved network liquidity, with policy-set vesting of 24 months.
\item \textbf{40\% --- public-liquidity reserve:} held in a timelocked governance vault for optional external-market and accessibility programs.
\end{itemize}
Under the illustrative public-liquidity controls:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item no more than 10\% of total supply would be active across external venues at one time;
\item each deployment would expire after 90 days unless renewed through the adopted process;
\item liquidity positions and any position tokens would remain under disclosed governance control; and
\item proposed CLC governance tokens used in liquidity positions would not vote unless locked under the same rules as other voting tokens.
\end{itemize}
A future launch could begin with a disclosed multisig and transition only after separately approved hardening milestones, such as independent audits, monitoring, incident runbooks, and tested pause and fork procedures. Every adopted parameter and change process would be published separately.

\subsubsection*{7.2.2 Availability and endowment stages}

A future endowment program could use staged contribution windows and a published reference value for intake and budgeting. That reference would not be a promise of market price, appreciation, repayment, or exit.

Contribution terms would state whether assets are donated, endowed, withdrawable, or repayable; who controls them; their permitted use; lockups; loss allocation; reporting; and exit conditions. Large contributions could face voting caps or diminishing governance weight.

Any external-market liquidity would require a separate decision identifying venues, responsible operators, assets, caps, timing, conflicts, risk controls, reporting, and termination. The policy would not target or guarantee a price.

\subsubsection*{7.2.3 Proposed impact-seeding program}

A future program could allocate part of the governance vault to contributors who place assets into designated Commitment Pools and measurably improve exchange access and confirmed issuer fulfillment.

An adopted eligibility policy would:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item identify approved Pools, assets, minimum duration, and lockup terms;
\item define productive contribution using executed-swap evidence and separately evidenced presentment, fulfillment, and discharge;
\item measure marginal contribution rather than total value locked alone;
\item exclude self-dealing and circular wash activity;
\item apply per-entity caps and diminishing returns; and
\item provide an observation, dispute, and correction window before final allocation.
\end{enumerate}
Any grant would remain subject to the program's published terms, vesting, eligibility, compliance, and loss rules.

\subsection*{7.3 Proposed voting, incentives, and fee access}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{7.3 Proposed voting, incentives, and fee access}

Under this design, stCLC holders could vote on eligible Pools, route-service mandates, shared budgets, or other adopted proposals. A curated gauge system could translate votes into bounded sCLC incentives for eligible liquidity or routing operators.

The measurement policy would keep executed swaps and issuer fulfillment separate. It would not reward quoted but unexecuted routes, unresolved presentments, self-dealing, or total value locked without evidence of useful activity.

An adopted governance process could also publish an epoch fee-credit budget. Eligible stCLC holders might receive sCLC authorizing capped, time-limited swaps from designated fee-holding Pools into allowlisted assets or programs. This would be policy-gated access to available inventory, not passive income or ownership of the fee-holding Pool.

\subsubsection*{7.3.1 Service-fee enforcement and profile choice}

A future registry profile could require participating Pools or route services to use a compatible fee adapter or hook. The profile might refuse discovery, routing, or program support when the required receipt does not prove fee collection.

Names such as \textbf{PoolFactory} or \textbf{FeeHook} describe possible future modules; they are not Protocol v1.1.0 contracts. Any implementation would disclose the code, addresses, controllers, recipients, rates, eligible transactions, failure handling, and audit status.

Enforcement would be profile-specific. A Pool excluded by one profile could continue operating locally or join another compatible registry if its contracts and dependencies remained functional. Clients should expose available profiles and show applicable fees before authorization.

\subsubsection*{7.3.2 sCLC budget and optional external-float controls}

After funding adopted higher-priority budgets, a future process could publish an epoch fee-credit budget \texttt{F\_epoch}, including zero. A policy might assign a participant limit in proportion to stCLC voting power:

\[
\mathrm{limit}_{\mathrm{user,epoch}} = F_{\mathrm{epoch}} \times \frac{\mathrm{stCLC}_{\mathrm{user}}}{\mathrm{stCLC}_{\mathrm{total}}}
\]

Every window would remain subject to allowlists, caps, inventory, eligibility, incident rules, and applicable law.

A separate policy could authorize a capped, time-weighted acquisition of the proposed CLC governance token only to reduce a measured external governance-attack surface. It would require:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item a published trigger based on external float over a stated period;
\item a maximum share of realized, eligible fees and venue volume;
\item time-weighted execution with no exact timing announcement;
\item an automatic stop when adopted coverage or operating thresholds are not met;
\item retirement or placement of acquired tokens in a disclosed non-voting account; and
\item an explicit statement that the program does not target or guarantee a price and does not affect issuer fulfillment.
\end{itemize}
The policy could remain disabled indefinitely.

\subsubsection*{7.3.3 Portfolio Pools and attestations}

A \textbf{portfolio Pool} would be an ordinary Commitment Pool curated around a mission or service domain. Its Pool Steward could be an individual, cooperative, community group, public agency, federation, multisig, service operator, or another accountable structure.

Support could occur through:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item direct contributions under the Pool's published terms;
\item time-bounded liquidity mandates approved through the adopted governance process; or
\item sCLC-directed access to a bounded post-Waterfall budget when that mechanism is enabled.
\end{enumerate}
Portfolio Pools would remain independently governed and could use shared or independent registries.

Certifications could inform admission or risk treatment but would not create entitlement to fees, profit, or residual assets. A deployment might use:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{registry-bound attestations} from an identified verifier applying a published method; or
\item \textbf{verification-service vouchers} representing a redeemable commitment to perform a stated audit or assessment.
\end{itemize}
The Pool would disclose how an attestation affects admission, exchange-rate methods, limits, or eligibility and how errors, expiry, conflicts, and appeals are handled.

\subsection*{7.4 Proposed Waterfall and budgets}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{7.4 Proposed Waterfall and budgets}

The proposed Waterfall would allocate only realized, eligible receipts under an adopted governance process. It would distinguish:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{cash-eligible assets (\texttt{E\_cash}):} allowlisted fungible assets that may be used or converted under policy for cash-denominated obligations; and
\item \textbf{in-kind assets (\texttt{E\_kind}):} vouchers or other assets that may support in-network exchange but do not count toward cash-denominated coverage or operating obligations unless actually converted.
\end{itemize}
A conversion policy would identify authorized operators, assets, venues, price sources, time windows, slippage bounds, caps, conflicts, records, and incident procedures. Treasury conversion would not determine an issuer's voucher obligation or a Pool's exchange-rate method.

An illustrative priority order is:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Funded coverage target:} build any adopted reserve or proposed insurance fund to its disclosed target for defined covered events.
\item \textbf{Core operations:} fund a capped, disclosed budget for legal work, education, communications, infrastructure, audits, and observability.
\item \textbf{Liquidity mandates:} allocate approved assets to stated Pool or route-service purposes under caps and sunset review.
\item \textbf{Optional external-float control:} fund only when its published trigger and higher-priority thresholds are satisfied; otherwise allocate zero.
\item \textbf{Proposed fee-credit budget:} allocate any approved remainder to designated fee-holding Pools and publish \texttt{F\_epoch}, which may be zero.
\end{enumerate}
Budget decisions could consider confirmed fulfillment, covered exposure, eligible reserves, limit utilization, executed routes, concentration, incidents, and guarantor performance. Each metric would follow the evidence and cohort rules in Appendix C.

A possible contributor flow would be:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item contributors transfer eligible assets under separately adopted endowment or program terms;
\item eligible participants receive and, where permitted, lock proposed CLC governance tokens to obtain stCLC;
\item realized eligible rake or service-fee receipts enter the Waterfall;
\item the Waterfall funds adopted priorities in order; and
\item only an enabled post-Waterfall policy issues sCLC or opens a capped fee-credit window.
\end{enumerate}
No step creates ownership, repayment, withdrawal, coverage, reward, or governance rights beyond those expressly stated in the applicable terms.


\section*{8. Technical scope and growth}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{8. Technical scope and growth}

This chapter describes optional or proposed work. It is not a list of features guaranteed to be present in the current CLC App or Protocol v1.1.0.

Possible work areas include:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item execution routing across compatible Pools, with registry, quote, limit, fee, and inventory discovery;
\item timelocked escrow or HTLC adapters for cross-domain execution where atomic settlement is unavailable;
\item interfaces and policy tools for small or personal Pools;
\item auditable registries for vouchers, Pools, exchange-rate methods, limits, controllers, and fees;
\item deployment-specific payment-provider connectors, checkout flows, and eligibility controls;
\item fungible-asset connections to external liquidity venues for rebalancing and payment liquidity; and
\item policy-capped treasury conversion for adopted coverage, operating costs, or liquidity mandates.
\end{itemize}
External-market prices would not determine what an issuer owes under voucher terms. A Pool could use a guarded external reference for a fungible asset, but its published exchange-rate method, limits, fees, and inventory would govern its quotes.

\subsection*{8.1 Proposed route-service and SDK norms}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{8.1 Proposed route-service and SDK norms}

\textbf{Discovery.} A proposed route service would query identified registries for asset admission, exchange-rate methods, limits, fees, inventory, incidents, and controller information. Cached records would include freshness bounds and source identifiers.

\textbf{Network profiles.} A client could support more than one registry root or policy profile. It would tell the participant which profile, counterparties, adapters, constraints, and responsible service operators a quote uses. A cross-profile route would need to satisfy every applicable hop's conditions.

\textbf{Path policy.} An accountable operator could exclude unsafe dependencies or counterparties and apply route-level caps, freshness requirements, and health criteria. These signals would support a decision; they would not guarantee fulfillment or protection from loss.

\textbf{Fees and limits.} A quote would itemize Pool fees, any additional current Protocol fee, and any separately proposed routing or service fee. Execution would reject expired quotes or breached bounds.

\textbf{Atomicity and recovery.} Multi-hop execution would be atomic where possible. Where it used HTLCs or escrow, the service would disclose timeouts, abort paths, responsible controllers, incident procedures, and residual risks.

\textbf{Proposed batch netting and rebalancing.} An opt-in service might collect rebalance intents and search for compatible cycles or chains. It would:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item publish a machine-readable receipt identifying executed cycles, assets, amounts, valuation timestamps, and fees;
\item enforce adopted per-period caps and counterparty policies;
\item reject activity that breaches any participating Pool's authorization, limits, or available inventory; and
\item preserve deterministic inputs and receipts for review and dispute handling.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{SDK requirements.} An SDK for executed routes would provide deterministic quote-to-receipt mapping, per-hop invariant checks, understandable failure codes, and audit-friendly logs. The current Protocol v1.1.0 \texttt{SwapRouter} provides quotes only; it does not execute these proposed routes.

\subsubsection*{8.1.1 Minimum confederation compatibility specification}

A Pool ecosystem seeking cross-profile routing would publish machine-readable information for:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Registry roots:} identifiers for assets, Pools, exchange-rate methods, limits, and fee policies, or one root that deterministically resolves them.
\item \textbf{Receipts:} the profile, assets in and out, amounts, quote source and timestamp, limit snapshot, fees, inventory result, and execution outcome for every hop.
\item \textbf{Operational signals:} freshness-bounded information about inventory, limit utilization, incidents, and any separately evidenced fulfillment or funded protection.
\item \textbf{Policy constraints:} allowed or denied counterparties, asset classes, adapters, and any escrow requirements.
\item \textbf{Failure codes:} deterministic explanations for rejection, expiry, limit, inventory, policy, dependency, or incident failures.
\end{enumerate}
A profile could add coverage, compliance, arbitration, or other services without making them requirements for basic CPP compatibility. Each optional service would identify its responsible party, authority, scope, and terms.

\subsection*{8.2 Licensing, verification, and exit}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{8.2 Licensing, verification, and exit}

Protocol v1.1.0 contracts are EVM-compatible. Contracts in the Protocol repository's \texttt{src} directory are published under AGPL-3.0 except for identified unmodified third-party components that retain their own terms. Published source, ABIs, and deployment instructions support independent review but do not by themselves prove an audit, safe deployment, or legal compliance.

Each deployment would separately disclose its code version, build provenance, addresses, controller and upgrade powers, audit status, registry mirrors, and any timelock or pause protections.

A proposed \textbf{fork kit} could include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item deterministic deployment scripts;
\item registry snapshot and export tools;
\item a documented process for repointing route services, SDKs, and interfaces to a new registry root;
\item a Pool Steward checklist for leaving a shared registry safely; and
\item a migration checklist for outstanding vouchers, including issuer notices, presentment and fulfillment deadlines, continued access to records, and remedies.
\end{enumerate}
Compatible forks can improve resilience when communities, cooperatives, public agencies, federations, multisigs, or service operators need different governance. Actual continuity still depends on contract ownership, keys, dependencies, interfaces, infrastructure, legal obligations, and third-party services.


\section*{9. Proposed economics for liquidity programs}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9. Proposed economics for liquidity programs}

This chapter describes a future, separately adopted model. It is not a current App feature, an offer, a promised return, or a right created by depositing into \texttt{SwapPool}.

\subsection*{9.1 Proposed revenue sources}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{9.1 Proposed revenue sources}

A future network budget could receive:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item a disclosed \textbf{network rake} taken as a share of participating Pools' collected fees;
\item separate routing or service fees from implemented shared services; and
\item other expressly adopted, received revenue.
\end{enumerate}
Gross Pool fees retained by Pools are not network revenue. Current Protocol v1.1.0 uses a different model: an optional protocol fee is additional to the Pool fee and is sent directly to its configured recipient.

\subsection*{9.2 Illustrative rake math}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{9.2 Illustrative rake math}

If a participating Pool charges a 2.00\% Pool fee and an adopted network rake receives 20\% of that Pool fee, the proposed effective network-rake rate on routed value is:

\texttt{rake\_rate = 2.00\% $\times$ 20\% = 0.40\% = 40 bps}

If 25\% of received rake and service-fee assets are eligible and convertible for a stated cash-denominated use after costs, the cash-usable share of the 40-bps rake is approximately 10 bps.

Proposed network revenue is:

\texttt{network\_rake\_received + routing\_or\_service\_fees\_received}

Do not add gross Pool fees to the network rake: the rake is a transfer from those fees and would otherwise be counted twice.

\subsection*{9.3 Liquidity-program rights}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{9.3 Liquidity-program rights}

A separately adopted program could fund Pool inventory, routing services, monitoring, or other mandates. Its terms would need to disclose:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item whether a transfer is a gift, endowment, loan, recoverable contribution, or purchase;
\item custody and control;
\item withdrawal, repayment, loss, and priority rules;
\item fee and reward eligibility;
\item governance rights;
\item valuation and reporting methods; and
\item suspension, termination, and remedies.
\end{itemize}
The current \texttt{SwapPool} creates no Pool-share token or automatic contributor entitlement. Any ex-post metric must be based on realized receipts and losses, must not be presented as promised yield, and may be zero or negative.


\section*{10. Comprehensive risk framework}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{10. Comprehensive risk framework}

This proposed framework separates ten risk categories. For each category it identifies possible indicators, controls, stress tests, and an indicative risk appetite. These are design recommendations, not claims that every control is deployed, effective, or sufficient. Limits, reserves, guarantees, monitoring, coverage, and governance cannot eliminate loss.

\subsection*{10.1 Protocol and smart-contract risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.1 Protocol and smart-contract risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} contract bugs, upgrade errors, dependency failures, and misconfigured limits or fees.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} audit findings, unexplained inventory movements, invariant failures, and unusual reverts.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} independent audits; minimal privileged roles; disclosed proxy and dependency controllers; timelocked upgrades; on-chain monitoring; incident pauses with published authority and criteria; and a tested migration path.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} unavailable quote or limit dependencies, inventory shortfalls, paused contracts, and burst traffic.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low before scaling outstanding obligations or swap volume.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.2 Economic and market risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.2 Economic and market risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} thin inventory, one-sided flows, rapid withdrawals, and manipulated or stale price references.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} high Pool token-balance-cap utilization, widening quote differences, frequent limit rejections, and concentrated inventory.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} current Pool token-balance caps; proposed rolling or account limits; separately adopted reserves; guarded price references; route exclusions; and time-limited incident fees or limits.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} large reference-price moves, presentment surges, withdrawal requests, and data-source outages.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} moderate only within published parameters and funded loss-bearing capacity.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.3 Issuer and voucher risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.3 Issuer and voucher risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} issuance beyond fulfillment capacity, issuer default, misleading terms, and poorly specified availability or presentment windows.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} declining confirmed-fulfillment rates, aging outstanding vouchers, unresolved complaints, and concentrated exposure to one issuer.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} issuer due diligence; clear voucher and Offering terms; issuance or admission limits; independently funded bonds or guarantees; cohort reporting; and accountable registry review.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} issuer insolvency, regional production shocks, counterfeit claims, and prolonged fulfillment delays.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} lower as issuer or Offering concentration increases.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.4 Redemption-presentment and fulfillment risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.4 Redemption-presentment and fulfillment risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} invalid or duplicate presentment, insufficient issuer capacity, stockouts, logistics failures, and missing discharge records.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} request-to-fulfillment latency, failed or disputed presentments, stockouts, ticket backlogs, and fulfilled units lacking discharge.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} published presentment and fulfillment procedures; capacity disclosures; multiple fulfillment venues where lawful; evidence standards; complaint and remedy paths; and records separating presentment, fulfillment, and discharge.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} two-to-four-times presentment volume, facility outages, supplier failures, and duplicate-use attempts.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low where essential goods, vulnerable participants, or long fulfillment windows are involved.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.5 Governance risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.5 Governance risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} Steward or controller capture, rushed parameter changes, conflicts of interest, hidden technical powers, and weak appeals.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} concentrated authority, frequent emergency actions, unexplained policy changes, and repeated overrides.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} role-and-power disclosures; proportional approval thresholds; timelocks; conflicts and recusal rules; public change records; appeals; automatic emergency-power sunsets; and forkability.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} adversarial proposals, signer loss, bribery attempts, and capture through accumulation or delegated control.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low for actions affecting value methods, withdrawals, registry roots, coverage, or emergency powers.
\end{itemize}
For a future deployment of the proposed CLC governance token, the capture test would include accumulation followed by attempts to redirect budgets, weaken registry standards, approve related-party mandates, or drain funded coverage. Possible safeguards include governance lockups, higher thresholds for critical actions, delayed execution, transparent delegation and concentration monitoring, an incident process, and a credible fork-and-exit procedure. None is represented as deployed merely by appearing here.

\subsection*{10.6 Legal and compliance risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.6 Legal and compliance risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} a voucher, service, promotion, or governance asset receiving unexpected regulatory treatment; consumer-protection failures; money-laundering or sanctions exposure; and cross-border restrictions.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} jurisdiction flags, regulator inquiries, complaints, restricted-party matches, and divergence between advertised and actual behavior.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} review by class and jurisdiction; accurate disclosures; geofenced interfaces; proportionate eligibility or attestation checks; promotion controls; records of authority and acceptance; and clear responsible parties.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} a jurisdictional restriction, provider termination, mandatory reclassification, and an order to pause a feature or asset class.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low; restrict or pause unsupported activity.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection*{10.6.1 Legal positioning and proposed-token treatment}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Verifiable infrastructure:} Protocol v1.1.0 contracts are EVM-compatible and their source is published under the licences and third-party exceptions identified in the Protocol repository. Publication enables review but does not itself prove an audit, safe deployment, or legal compliance. Each deployment should disclose its code version, build provenance, addresses, controller powers, and audit status.
\item \textbf{Proposed-token posture:} in this design, the proposed CLC governance token would coordinate governance and policy-gated access. It would not create dividends, profit sharing, residual rights, or a guarantee of value or liquidity.
\item \textbf{Related proposed assets:} a separately implemented policy might allow locking the proposed CLC governance token to mint stCLC and might authorize epoch-scoped sCLC. The adopted terms would need to define their exact rights, limits, expiry, transferability, and treatment.
\item \textbf{Communications:} materials for any proposed CLC governance-token, stCLC, or sCLC deployment should not promise profit, appreciation, passive income, or guaranteed access.
\item \textbf{Jurisdiction controls:} a deployment might require interface geofencing, attestations for restricted classes, promotion limits, asset-specific review, or controls that pause a proposed feature.
\item \textbf{Participant notice:} adopted terms would explain when access can be reduced or disabled for legal, operational, or risk reasons and whether any compensation or remedy applies.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{10.7 Routing and cross-domain risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.7 Routing and cross-domain risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} partial execution, stuck hops, bridge or escrow exploits, stale quotes, path inflation, and front-running of announced value changes.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} route-expiry rates, escrow backlogs, quote-to-execution differences, repeated unnecessary hops, and bridge incidents.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} atomic execution where available; conservative HTLC or escrow timeouts; path and counterparty policies; route-level caps; deterministic quote-to-receipt mapping; and accountable service operators.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} a bridge halt, chain reorganization, dependency outage, and one failed hop in a proposed multi-hop route.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low to moderate only for identified and monitored dependencies.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.8 Custody and key-management risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.8 Custody and key-management risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} lost or compromised keys, signer collusion, and unclear recovery or administrator authority.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} anomalous signatures, controller changes, failed rotations, and unusual withdrawals.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} multisig or threshold authorization; hardware-backed keys; role separation; signer rotation; public controller inventories; monitored withdrawal limits; and tested recovery procedures.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.9 Reputation and social risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.9 Reputation and social risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} misleading claims, harmful incentives, inaccessible complaints, poor fulfillment experiences, privacy failures, and protections that favor insiders.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} complaints by cohort, unresolved disputes, issuer-performance trends, concentration of benefits or losses, and community feedback.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} plain-language disclosures; grievance and correction paths; accessible evidence; transparent reporting; incident review; and proportionate sanctions for misrepresentation.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} low, with particular care for affected communities and vulnerable participants.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{10.10 Concentration and fragmentation risk}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{10.10 Concentration and fragmentation risk}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Threats:} dependence on a small number of issuers, Pools, controllers, providers, networks, or incompatible forks.
\item \textbf{Indicators:} concentration measures by issuer, Pool, inventory, controller, or service provider; route failures between clusters; and critical single dependencies.
\item \textbf{Possible controls:} published concentration thresholds; multiple accountable operators; compatible standards; independent registries; tested exit procedures; and safe interoperability.
\item \textbf{Stress tests:} loss of the largest issuer, Pool, operator, provider, or registry root.
\item \textbf{Risk appetite:} deployment-specific and disclosed, with stricter limits for essential services or irreplaceable dependencies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{11. Governance mechanics}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{11. Governance mechanics}

This chapter proposes a governance template. It does not represent that the current CLC App uses governance-token voting, timelocks, shared insurance, a claims process, or every control described below. Each deployment would need to identify its actual decision-makers, authorities, contracts, processes, and policies.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Constitutional values:} care for people, care for the environment, fairness, reciprocity, non-dominance, and resilience.
\item \textbf{Proposal types:} fee, limit, and index changes; liquidity mandates; Pool listings and removals; optional coverage decisions; and parameter guardrails.
\item \textbf{Accountable process:} intake $\to$ evaluation $\to$ risk review $\to$ approval $\to$ timelock where appropriate $\to$ execution. Approval may come from stewards, cooperatives, public agencies, federations, multisigs, on-chain voting, or another disclosed and accountable structure.
\item \textbf{Approval thresholds:} parameterized by action class, with higher thresholds for value-index changes, emergency powers, and other critical actions.
\item \textbf{Delegation:} optional delegation with public mandates, conflicts disclosures, and recall.
\item \textbf{Circuit breakers:} emergency pauses with stated criteria, authorized operators, resume conditions, and required post-mortems.
\item \textbf{Transparency:} published changes and flows, with separate evidence for swap settlement, issuer fulfillment, reserves, limit utilization, routing, and guarantors.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Registry governance.} A CPP-compatible deployment may maintain discovery registries for vouchers, tokens, and Pools. Authorized controls may add, update, suspend, or remove registry entries through the deployment's disclosed governance process. Registry removal affects discovery and routing through that registry; it does not by itself erase a token, alter a holder's balance, discharge an issuer's obligation, or disable an otherwise functional contract.

Published registry rules should make status conditional and may identify repeated non-fulfillment, fraud or misrepresentation, unsafe contract behavior, or persistent violation of published principles as grounds for suspension or removal. Where feasible, the process should provide notice, an opportunity to remedy, and an appeal path. Emergency removal should require a public incident report and automatic review or sunset.

\textbf{Prohibited listings.} Under this template, a registry would not admit:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item instruments that directly fund or incentivize ecological destruction beyond agreed boundaries, violence or weaponization, coercive extraction, or systemic abuse; or
\item a voucher class that lacks clear presentment and fulfillment terms, accountability, and remedy paths.
\end{enumerate}
The prohibited list would be versioned, publicly auditable, and changeable only through the adopted critical-action threshold and timelock, illustrated as Q3 + T3 in Appendix D.

\subsection*{11.1 Exchange-rate and limit governance}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{11.1 Exchange-rate and limit governance}

\textbf{Timelocked changes.} A deployment following this template would change exchange-rate methods and separately implemented limit parameters only after a public timelock. An emergency path would use a separately disclosed authorization process and include an automatic sunset or review.

\textbf{Approval thresholds.} The template proposes higher approval thresholds for value-index base changes and global limit-tier changes, intermediate thresholds for Pool-specific third-party changes, and standard thresholds for routine fee changes.

\textbf{Published feeds.} A participating deployment would publish, for each Pool, the on-chain index variables, oracle sources or medians, update cadence, limit windows and caps, and failure modes or safe constants.

\textbf{Emergency-pause criteria.} A participating deployment would predeclare conditions such as an oracle outage, high limit utilization combined with fulfillment failures, or an invariant failure, together with resume checks and post-incident review requirements.

\subsection*{Example public index feed for one Pool and voucher}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Example public index feed for one Pool and voucher}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Symbol:} for example, \texttt{Maize\_50kg@IssuerY}.
\item \textbf{Reference unit:} Index Unit (IUX).
\item \textbf{Published value:} 30.000 IUX.
\item \textbf{Source:} median of identified sources, such as a local market survey, ministry bulletin, and deployment baseline.
\item \textbf{Update cadence:} daily at 18:00 EAT, with a 24-hour timelock.
\item \textbf{Failure mode:} freeze at the last valid value, apply a disclosed limit policy, and pause after a 72-hour outage.
\item \textbf{Rationale:} published notes and a change record from the prior update.
\item \textbf{Signers:} disclosed multisig addresses and approval threshold.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{11.2 Proposed insurance-fund runbook}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{11.2 Proposed insurance-fund runbook}

\textbf{Optional design only.} This runbook applies only to a deployment that has expressly adopted and funded an insurance fund and published the covered events, eligible claimants, responsible entity, assets, limits, exclusions, evidence requirements, process, and governing terms. Neither the current CLC App nor GEF provides coverage merely because this design appears in the White Paper.

\textbf{Possible triggers.} An adopted policy could cover defined issuer non-fulfillment, a Pool reserve shortfall, or a bridge or escrow loss. A technical incident does not qualify automatically; the applicable policy would control.

\textbf{Assessment.} The responsible body would reconcile transaction receipts, inventory balances, guarantor bonds, redemption-presentment records, issuer responses, and other required evidence, then publish an incident record consistent with privacy and law.

\textbf{Illustrative loss waterfall.} Where each layer exists and lawfully applies, a policy could use: (1) responsible issuer bonds or guarantor stakes $\to$ (2) Pool-level reserves $\to$ (3) a proposed network insurance fund $\to$ (4) a temporary reduction to an optional coverage claim, only where pre-existing terms and applicable law expressly authorize it $\to$ (5) lawful recovery for proven fraud or abuse.

A coverage adjustment does not reduce an issuer's underlying voucher commitment or alter an on-chain balance unless valid pre-existing terms and applicable law expressly permit that result and any required holder consent is obtained.

\textbf{Limits and exclusions.} Published coverage would define caps, eligible presentments, evidence, claim windows, excluded routes or events, geographic restrictions, and the treatment of exhausted reserves. A payout could be zero after applicable limits are reached.

\textbf{Illustrative recovery schedule.} If adopted and published:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item claims would draw first from the responsible issuer or guarantor bond, then from applicable Pool reserves, then from the proposed network insurance fund;
\item any reduction to an optional coverage claim would be limited to what pre-existing coverage terms and applicable law authorize, up to the published incident cap;
\item a recovery plan could apply a stated share of recovered value for a stated period, after which any remaining covered shortfall would become a recorded loss with a public post-mortem; and
\item each decision would produce a receipt with the incident ID, affected claim and vouchers, decision, recovery plan, and appeal window.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{11.3 Guarantor framework}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{11.3 Guarantor framework}

This section distinguishes issuer responsibility, optional Pool protections, and third-party guarantees. Pools can compete on curation, terms, and expressly offered protections without implying that the CLC App, CPP, GEF, or any wider network automatically guarantees a voucher.

\subsection*{Baseline issuer responsibility}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Baseline issuer responsibility}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Each voucher is first and foremost its issuer's responsibility. The issuer commits to provide the stated good, service, or lawful cash-equivalent under its published terms.
\item Issuers would publish who may present the voucher, what fulfillment means, where and when it is available, what evidence is required, and what remedies apply.
\item If an issuer fails to fulfill, the issuer is the primary responsible party. Pool or network protections apply only when separately adopted, funded, and disclosed.
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{Optional Pool protections}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Optional Pool protections}

A Pool Steward may choose to add a narrowly defined protection to admitted vouchers. It is not automatic and would need to identify the responsible party, funding, eligible events, caps, windows, evidence, exclusions, and remedies in Pool metadata and applicable terms.

Illustrative protection types include:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Reserve-asset coverage:} after verified issuer non-fulfillment, the responsible Pool entity pays a defined amount in a designated reserve asset, subject to its published cap and available funded reserves.
\item \textbf{Swap-back window:} after a qualifying event, the Pool offers a time-limited swap path into the prior or another approved asset, subject to caps and inventory. This is an inventory-dependent liquidity protection, not a promise that every swap is reversible.
\item \textbf{Alternative fulfillment:} the responsible party arranges an approved substitute provider within a published quantity or value cap.
\item \textbf{Exchange-rate-band protection:} for selected voucher classes, a Pool offers only the coverage adjustment or swap-back remedy stated in its pre-existing terms. This does not reduce the issuer's underlying voucher obligation.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{Possible funding sources}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Possible funding sources}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Issuer bond:} collateral posted by the issuer or held in a disclosed reserve and available after a verified covered event.
\item \textbf{Pool reserve:} assets controlled by the responsible Pool entity and allocated to the protections it advertises.
\item \textbf{Third-party guarantor bond:} collateral posted by an identified external guarantor for stated issuers, voucher classes, or events.
\end{itemize}
Guarantor participation would follow published eligibility criteria, bond sizing, concentration limits, decision authority, and lawful enforcement rules.

\subsection*{Claims process}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Claims process}

An adopted policy would define auditable triggers, such as a fulfillment deadline missed after valid redemption presentment, verified issuer insolvency, a covered bridge or escrow failure, or a formally declared incident state. It would also define:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item how a participant opens a claim and supplies the required presentment and fulfillment evidence;
\item who verifies voucher terms, issuer responses, and technical records;
\item the decision and appeal windows; and
\item the authorized payout path, assets, caps, and receipt.
\end{itemize}
Recovery proceeds from issuers, arbitration, or lawful enforcement would refill the applicable bonds or reserves according to the published policy before being used for proposed CLC Network Pool swap access.

\subsection*{Required disclosures}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Required disclosures}

For every covered Pool and voucher class, the responsible party would publish:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item whether a guarantor is absent, optional, or required;
\item bond or reserve sizing and concentration caps;
\item the protection types, assets, caps, windows, and exclusions;
\item presentment, fulfillment, claim, and appeal deadlines; and
\item a plain-language statement of who guarantees what and what is not guaranteed.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Curation principle.} Pool Stewards and the responsible legal or governance structures are accountable for the protections they advertise. A CPP-compatible deployment may provide standards, registries, or optional shared policies, but neither CLC nor GEF automatically guarantees vouchers or Pools.

\subsection*{11.4 Anti-capture guardrails}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{11.4 Anti-capture guardrails}

Under this template, the following would be critical actions requiring the adopted highest approval tier and a long timelock:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item changing the proposed fee waterfall, including its coverage and core-operations priorities;
\item changing canonical registry roots;
\item changing coverage scope, claim caps, or decision authority;
\item expanding emergency-pause powers; or
\item weakening forkability, transparency, or Pool sovereignty commitments stated in this paper.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{11.5 Fork and exit procedure}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{11.5 Fork and exit procedure}

If governance were captured or values drifted materially, communities, Pool Stewards, and operators could seek to exit by forking the network governance layer. Continuity of underlying Pools and vouchers would depend on the deployed contracts, keys, interfaces, infrastructure, third-party services, and applicable obligations.

An exit process could:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Publish a snapshot:} export the selected registries, vouchers, values, limits, and fee policies, then publish a signed snapshot hash.
\item \textbf{Redeploy governance services:} deploy new registry roots, route services, and any adopted fee or coverage modules under a new accountable structure.
\item \textbf{Re-register:} allow Pool Stewards to opt in by registering their Pool addresses under the new root without requiring holders to migrate otherwise functional vouchers.
\item \textbf{Repoint clients:} add the new root as a selectable network profile in SDKs and interfaces, with any default change made through the disclosed governance process.
\item \textbf{Manage a bridge period:} maintain compatible routes where safe and deny routes that violate the new profile's rules.
\end{enumerate}
The design objective is that leaving a canonical registry does not disable otherwise functional local Pools. Actual continuity remains deployment-dependent; federation is an opt-in discovery and coordination layer.

\subsection*{17.3 Actions and obligations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{17.3 Actions and obligations}

An ordinary \textbf{Pool swap} exchanges supported assets under a displayed quote and transaction bounds. Asset direction does not make it a loan, repayment, issuer redemption, or real-world fulfillment.

\textbf{Redemption presentment} returns or presents voucher units to an issuer. \textbf{Fulfillment} is the issuer's promised performance. \textbf{Discharge} records fulfilled units so they cannot be reused. The App's \textbf{``Redeem''} action currently prepares a transfer to the token owner; that transfer alone does not prove fulfillment.

The App's \textbf{``Retire voucher''} action is reversible catalog unlisting. It does not burn balances, cancel claims, or discharge obligations.

A future loan or other credit product would require separately presented supplemental and transaction terms before use. No ordinary send, contribution, Pool deposit, Pool swap, presentment, fulfillment, or discharge creates a loan merely because of its direction or asset type.

\subsection*{17.4 Protections, evidence, and local law}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{17.4 Protections, evidence, and local law}

A limit, reserve, registry entry, App listing, or blockchain transaction is not automatically a guarantee or insurance policy. Any protection must identify its responsible party, covered event, funding, cap, exclusions, duration, evidence, claim process, and applicable terms.

Public-chain evidence can show addresses, assets, amounts, timestamps, and contract events. It does not by itself prove identity, issuer capacity, fulfillment, satisfaction, governance deliberation, legal discharge, or social impact.

Features may be restricted or unavailable because of law, sanctions, eligibility, contract state, inventory, limits, security, jurisdiction, or third-party services. Issuers, Pool Stewards, providers, and participants remain responsible for determining and complying with applicable law.

\section*{A. Proposed measurement and fee model}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{A. Proposed measurement and fee model}

This appendix defines a proposed measurement framework. Protocol v1.1.0 does not record issuer fulfillment, real-world discharge, or every data field required below.

\subsection*{Event and stock definitions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Event and stock definitions}

For voucher class \emph{j}, cohort or period \emph{t}, and a disclosed valuation method \emph{m}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{O\_\{j,t,m\}}: value of outstanding eligible commitments at the measurement boundary.
\item \texttt{X\_\{j,t,m\}}: value of completed Pool swaps during the period.
\item \texttt{P\_\{j,t\}}: units validly presented to the issuer for redemption.
\item \texttt{F\_\{j,t\}}: presented units with separately evidenced issuer fulfillment.
\item \texttt{G\_\{j,t\}}: fulfilled units with a discharge record preventing reuse.
\end{itemize}
\texttt{O} cannot be inferred from token supply alone. A measurement policy must identify the responsible issuer and exclude, as applicable, issuer-held inventory, burned units, expired units, discharged units, test balances, inaccessible balances, and tokens whose terms do not create an outstanding third-party commitment.

Each valued measure must publish the unit, source, valuation method, timestamp, and treatment of divergent Pool exchange rates. An on-chain transfer can support \texttt{X} or presentment evidence; it does not by itself establish \texttt{F} or \texttt{G}.

\subsection*{Cohort-based fulfillment measures}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Cohort-based fulfillment measures}

For a cohort of valid redemption presentments:

\texttt{FulfillmentRate = FulfilledPresentments / ValidPresentments}

\texttt{DischargeCompleteness = DischargedFulfillments / FulfilledPresentments}

Use the same closed or mature cohort in each numerator and denominator. Report rejected, withdrawn, expired, disputed, partially fulfilled, corrected, and still-open presentments separately.

\texttt{FulfillmentLatency = median(t\_fulfilled - t\_presented)}

\texttt{HoldingDuration = median(t\_presented - t\_acquired)}

Fulfillment latency measures issuer service after presentment. Holding duration is a separate measure and must not be labelled redemption latency.

\subsection*{Distinct velocity measures}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Distinct velocity measures}

A proposed commitment-discharge velocity may be calculated only where \texttt{O} and fulfilled value use the same valuation method:

\texttt{V\_commit,t = FulfilledValue\_t / AverageOutstandingEligibleCommitmentValue\_t}

A Pool swap-activity measure is separate:

\texttt{V\_swap,t = PoolSwapValue\_t / AverageMeasuredPoolInventoryValue\_t}

Neither value proves social impact, issuer capacity, profitability, or cash convertibility.

\subsection*{Proposed network revenue}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Proposed network revenue}

Let:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{PF\_t} be gross Pool fees generated during the period;
\item \texttt{NR\_t} be the proposed network rake actually received as a disclosed share of those Pool fees;
\item \texttt{RF\_t} be separate proposed routing or service fees actually received; and
\item \texttt{$\chi$\_t} be the measured share of received revenue that is eligible and convertible for a stated cash-denominated use after costs and policy constraints.
\end{itemize}
From the proposed network-budget perspective:

\texttt{ProposedNetworkRevenue\_t = NR\_t + RF\_t}

\texttt{CashUsableNetworkRevenue\_t = $\chi$\_t $\times$ (NR\_t + RF\_t)}

Do not add \texttt{PF\_t} to \texttt{NR\_t}: the rake is a transfer from gross Pool fees and would otherwise be counted twice. Current Protocol v1.1.0 instead supports an additional protocol fee; its receipts must be reported separately from this proposed rake model.


\section*{B. Illustrative future fee waterfall}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{B. Illustrative future fee waterfall}

This is a proposed design for a future deployment. It applies only if implemented, funded, and adopted through published governance and participant terms. It does not describe a current Protocol v1.1.0 fee, contributor right, reserve, guarantee, or insurance arrangement.

Let \texttt{F\_in} be revenue actually received by the proposed network budget during an epoch: the proposed network rake, separate routing or service fees, and any other expressly designated revenue. Gross Pool fees that remain with Pools are not included.

Revenue assets must be classified as:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \texttt{E\_cash}: assets eligible for a stated cash-denominated use under the adopted policy; or
\item \texttt{E\_kind}: in-kind vouchers or other assets that are not treated as cash-convertible.
\end{itemize}
Any conversion policy would need asset and venue allowlists, responsible authorities, price sources, slippage limits, reporting, and applicable legal controls.

A proposed waterfall could apply received revenue in this order:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Covered reserve target:} fund an adopted reserve or insurance target based only on defined covered exposure, eligible assets, exclusions, and claim rules.
\item \textbf{Core operations:} fund a disclosed, capped operating budget.
\item \textbf{Liquidity mandates:} fund approved, separately governed Pool or routing programs.
\item \textbf{Remaining budget:} allocate any remaining amount among operations, liquidity programs, and an additional buffer under published caps.
\end{enumerate}
A reserve target is not itself a guarantee. Any insurance or guarantee must identify the obligated party, covered event, funding, cap, exclusions, duration, evidence, claim process, and loss allocation.

\textbf{Guardrail:} Waterfall allocations are intended for adopted coverage, operations, and liquidity services. They must not be framed or executed as price-support operations.

Under the proposed model, any proposed CLC governance tokens acquired through a separately enabled external-liquidity program would be retired or placed in a disclosed non-voting sink. This mechanism is not deployed.

\section*{E. Worked example --- proposed network rake}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{E. Worked example --- proposed network rake}

This example illustrates the proposed rake model. It is not the additional protocol-fee calculation used by Protocol v1.1.0.

Assume:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item a participating Pool charges a 2.00\% Pool fee;
\item the proposed network rake receives 20\% of that Pool fee; and
\item 25\% of the received rake is eligible and convertible for a stated cash-denominated use after costs.
\end{itemize}
The effective proposed network-rake rate on routed value is:

\texttt{rake\_rate = 2.00\% $\times$ 20\% = 0.40\% = 40 bps}

The cash-usable portion under the assumed 25\% eligibility factor is:

\texttt{cash\_usable\_rate = 40 bps $\times$ 25\% = 10 bps}

If a proposed network budget has a monthly cash-denominated requirement of \$150,000 and no other revenue, the illustrative routed value required at a 10-bps cash-usable rate is:

\texttt{required\_routed\_value = \$150,000 / 0.001 = \$150,000,000 per month}

This calculation does not include gross Pool fees retained by Pools. Adding those fees to the network rake would double-count the rake source.

A real budget analysis would also need to publish:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item actual rake and service-fee receipts;
\item asset eligibility and conversion costs;
\item Pool participation and route scope;
\item adopted reserve and operating targets;
\item losses, disputes, corrections, and unavailable assets; and
\item sensitivity scenarios rather than promised growth or returns.
\end{itemize}
Any proposed fee-access or liquidity-program budget would remain downstream of its adopted priorities and could be zero.
